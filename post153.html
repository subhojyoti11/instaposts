Caption
The classical internet moves data by copying it. A signal travels a few kilometres down a fiber, weakens, and a repeater reads the bits, regenerates a clean copy, and sends it on. That simple trick, copy and boost, is what lets a bit cross an ocean. None of it works for a qubit. The no-cloning theorem forbids making a copy of an unknown quantum state, and any attempt to amplify a faint quantum signal disturbs it and destroys the information. So firing a single photon carrying a qubit down a long fiber fails: it is lost to attenuation long before it arrives, and nothing along the way is allowed to help. The quantum internet gets around this by changing what the network distributes. Instead of transmitting qubits, it distributes entanglement: it establishes shared entangled pairs between distant nodes. Once two nodes hold an entangled pair, one of them can send a qubit's state to the other by quantum teleportation, consuming the pair and a couple of ordinary classical bits sent over the normal internet. The same shared entanglement also underpins provably secure key exchange and lets separate small quantum processors act together as one larger machine. Building it needs quantum repeaters that work nothing like classical ones. Rather than reading and resending, they use entanglement swapping: entangle node A with B and B with C, then a measurement at B leaves A and C entangled without B ever touching the payload. Quantum memory holds each link stable while neighbouring segments catch up, and entanglement purification trades several noisy pairs for one cleaner one. The hard parts are exactly those components: quantum memories with long enough coherence, and efficient interfaces between photons and matter. Small multi-node testbeds exist, but a wide-area quantum internet is still years out. The network was never going to carry your qubit across the world. It carries entanglement, and your qubit steps across that. #quantuminternet #quantumcomputing #networking #entanglement #ravenextechnologies
