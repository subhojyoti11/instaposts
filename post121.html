Caption
Planes land in fog so thick the pilots genuinely cannot see the runway, and the aircraft does it by flying two invisible radio beams instead of looking for anything at all. Landing by sight works fine in clear weather, but it fundamentally requires the pilot to actually spot the runway before touchdown, which becomes impossible the moment visibility drops in dense fog. GPS-based approaches help refine a plane's position in space, but satellite positioning alone still isn't precise enough, by itself, to guarantee a safe touchdown when the runway is completely invisible. What actually enables a zero-visibility landing is the Instrument Landing System, a pair of radio transmitters positioned on the ground near the runway. A localizer transmitter broadcasts the correct left-right alignment with the runway centerline, while a separate glideslope transmitter broadcasts the correct vertical descent angle. The aircraft's autopilot continuously measures its position relative to both beams and steers to stay exactly where they intersect, correcting constantly rather than following a single fixed path. In the final seconds before touchdown, a radar altimeter measures the plane's true height above the actual ground below it, not estimated altitude from air pressure, and once that height crosses a threshold, the system automatically reduces engine power and gently pitches the nose up to flare for a soft landing, all without a hand on the controls. The highest certified category of this system, CAT IIIc, requires no minimum visual reference at all, meaning the aircraft can land with the runway entirely obscured by fog. Autoland-capable aircraft run three independent flight computers simultaneously, each voting on every control decision, so a single computer fault can't silently cause an unsafe correction. Pilots still actively monitor the entire automated approach and remain ready to intervene, even with their hands off the controls the whole time. Nobody was flying blind.
