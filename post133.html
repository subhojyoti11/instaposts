Caption
Most cancer drugs are given systemically, released into the bloodstream to circulate through the entire body in the hope that enough of the dose reaches the tumor. Only a small fraction actually does. The rest is absorbed by healthy tissue, which is what produces the side effects, and those side effects are what cap how much drug a patient can safely be given. The tumor is a small, specific target, and a pill has no way to aim. Medical microrobotics tries to solve the aiming problem directly. The carriers are microscopic, small enough to travel through blood vessels down to capillary size, and most designs contain no motor or battery at all. Instead they are built from a magnetically responsive material and loaded with the drug. Once injected near the target, they are moved by magnetic fields generated by equipment outside the patient's body. Carefully shaped and rotated fields can push, pull, and spin the carriers, driving them along a chosen path, and medical imaging tracks the swarm as it goes. When the carriers reach the tumor, the payload is released by a trigger rather than a timer, a small rise in temperature, the slightly acidic environment around a tumor, or an external pulse of ultrasound or light. That keeps the drug packaged until it is in the right place. The reason this is still largely in the laboratory and early trials is control and cleanup. Steering a large group of tiny carriers through flowing blood, against currents and around branches, is difficult, and once the job is done the carriers have to be cleared from the body safely, which is why many use biodegradable shells. The microbot was never a tiny machine with an engine inside. It is a speck of magnetic material carrying a drug, and everything that steers it is sitting outside the patient. #medtech #microrobotics #drugdelivery #biomedical #ravenextechnologies
