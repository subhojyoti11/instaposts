Three Fates for
a Satellite.

🛰️
ABANDON
Leave in orbit
Goal
Do nothing once fuel runs low or a part fails.
Focus
The spacecraft is simply left in its existing orbit.
Result
Hundreds of millions stranded, added debris risk.
🔥
DEORBIT
Controlled burn
Goal
Deliberately guide the satellite to burn up.
Focus
Uses the satellite's last fuel for a controlled descent.
Result
Removes debris risk, but the asset is still gone.
🔧
SERVICE
Repair in orbit
Goal
Dock with the satellite and extend its life.
Focus
A servicing spacecraft refuels or repairs it directly.
Result
Years of extra mission life from the same hardware.

07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

On-orbit servicing
Using a spacecraft to refuel, repair, or reposition another satellite while both remain in orbit.
Rendezvous
Matching orbits and closing distance with a target, typically over days rather than minutes.
Autonomous docking
Physically connecting two spacecraft without real-time control, necessary given orbital communication delay.
Space debris mitigation
Actively removing or deorbiting defunct satellites to reduce collision risk.

Caption
A satellite worth hundreds of millions of dollars used to become permanent space junk the moment it ran low on fuel, even if every other component still worked perfectly. On-orbit servicing exists because that was always an absurd way to lose an asset. For the entire history of spaceflight, satellites were built to be disposable in a very specific way, once deployed, nobody could touch them again. Run out of fuel, and a fully functional spacecraft becomes permanently stranded in its existing orbit, adding to a growing debris field that has already forced the International Space Station to maneuver out of the way of dangerous fragments. Deliberately deorbiting a dying satellite at least removes the collision risk, burning it up in the atmosphere using its last fuel reserves, but the asset itself is still gone for good either way. Autonomous servicing spacecraft change that equation by treating a stranded satellite as repairable rather than disposable. A servicing spacecraft autonomously matches the target's orbit and closes the distance over days, not minutes, since communication delay at orbital distances makes real-time joystick control physically impossible, the spacecraft has to make its own docking decisions. Cameras and lidar guide the final approach, docking with a satellite that in most cases was never even designed to be grabbed by anything. Once attached, robotic arms can transfer propellant, replace a failed component, or attach an entirely new propulsion module, largely without a human directly steering the process. This is no longer theoretical. Servicing spacecraft have already docked with and extended the operational life of commercial satellites by years, the first time that had ever been done in the industry. The same rendezvous and docking technology is now being adapted specifically to deorbit dead satellites and remove large debris before it fragments into thousands of smaller, far more dangerous pieces. A dead satellite used to just mean a dead satellite. Now it means a servicing mission hasn't reached it yet. #spacerobotics #onorbitservicing #satellites #spacetech #ravenextechnologies
