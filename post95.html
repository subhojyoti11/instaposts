Robots Couldn't
Generalize.

Hardcoded kinematics
Engineers wrote explicit inverse-kinematics equations for every joint angle. Change the object, and the equations no longer apply.
Zero transfer
A robot tuned to pick up one box shape had no ability to handle a differently shaped one, even a slightly rotated version of the same box.
The engineering cost
Months of manual tuning per task, starting over from scratch the moment the next task looked even slightly different.

Three Ways to
Move a Robot.

⚙️
RIGID
Kinematics
Goal
Hardcode exact joint trajectories by hand.
Focus
Inverse-kinematics equations solved per joint, per task.
Result
Breaks the moment the object or scene changes.
🎮
TELEOP
Human piloted
Goal
Capture human movement as training data.
Focus
VR headsets and haptic controllers driving a real robot.
Result
Rich, real-physics data, but one pilot per robot.
🧠
NEURAL
End-to-end
Goal
Learn a policy directly from demonstrations.
Focus
Vision-language-action models trained on teleop data.
Result
Generalizes to objects and tasks never seen before.

07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

Vision-Language-Action model
A single network that turns video and a text instruction directly into robot motor commands.
Teleoperation
A human remotely pilots a robot to generate training demonstrations, the raw data behind most humanoid policies.
Inverse kinematics
The classical, hand-derived math for computing joint angles from a desired end-effector position, brittle by nature.
End-to-end policy
A learned function mapping perception straight to action, with no hardcoded intermediate steps.

Caption
For decades, teaching a robot to pick something up meant an engineer writing the exact math by hand. That approach is dying, and humanoid robots like Figure 01 are the reason why. The classical method is rigid kinematics. An engineer derives explicit inverse-kinematics equations that convert a desired hand position into precise joint angles. It works, for exactly the object and scenario it was tuned for. Change the shape, rotate the object slightly, or ask the robot to do a different task, and those equations no longer apply. Months of tuning, thrown out the moment the environment shifts even a little. Teleoperation was the first real fix, not by removing the human, but by using them differently. A person remotely pilots the robot, often through a VR headset or motion controllers, and every movement gets logged as training data. It solves the generalization problem at the data level: instead of engineers writing equations for every scenario, you record humans actually doing the task, across as many variations as you can capture. The real shift is what happens with that data next. Instead of hardcoding anything, a vision-language-action model learns an end-to-end policy directly from those demonstrations. Show it a camera frame and a plain-language instruction, and it outputs motor commands, joint torques, gripper state, directly. No inverse kinematics equation ever runs. The robot generalizes to objects and tasks it never explicitly saw, because it learned the underlying skill, not a lookup table of trajectories. This is not a lab demo anymore. Figure AI has partnered with OpenAI to pair this kind of model with stronger language understanding, and Figure 01 has run real pilot tasks on an actual BMW factory floor in Spartanburg. Figure 02 pushed further, adding onboard cameras and microphones so the robot can perceive and hold a two-way conversation while it works. Rigid kinematics asked engineers to describe every motion. Teleoperation asked humans to demonstrate it. Neural policies just watch, and learn to act. #humanoidrobotics #figureai #robotics #embodiedai #machinelearning #ravenextechnologies
