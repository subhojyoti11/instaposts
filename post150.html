04 / 06

Bits to Bases
and Back.

Binary is mapped onto the four bases, with error correction and index tags added
Millions of short strands are chemically synthesized
The dried strand pool is sealed and stored cool
To read, the pool is sequenced and reassembled by its index tags

You don't spin a DNA drive. You copy the whole pool by amplifying it, sequence a sample, and the index tags tell software how to put the file back together.

Caption
The world creates data faster than affordable long-term storage can keep up, and every medium used for archives has the same two flaws: it physically degrades within a few decades, and the hardware to read its format disappears not long after. The practical consequence is that a serious archive is migrated forever, copied from tape to newer tape to disk to something else, every ten to fifteen years, at real cost, just to stay readable. DNA sidesteps both problems. It is an information storage molecule that biology has already proven can survive for thousands of years when kept cool and dry, and it is astonishingly dense, a single gram can in principle hold on the order of an exabyte. Crucially, the technology to read it, sequencing, will never become obsolete, because medicine and biology will always need it. Encoding works by mapping binary onto the four bases, A, C, G and T, roughly two bits per base before you subtract overhead for error correction and for avoiding sequences that are hard to synthesize or read, like long runs of the same base. The data is split into millions of short fragments, each tagged with an index that records where it belongs, and those fragments are chemically synthesized and stored as a dried pool. Reading means sequencing the pool, using the index tags to reassemble the original file, and running error correction over the result. The limitation is writing. Synthesizing DNA is slow and expensive compared to writing a disk, and retrieval is not random access in the way a drive is, you sequence and filter rather than seek. That makes DNA a fit for cold archival storage, write once and read rarely, not for working data. Costs are falling steadily but are still far from competitive. The archive was never going to last by being copied forever. It lasts by being written into the same kind of molecule that has stored information for billions of years. #dnastorage #datastorage #biotech #archives #ravenextechnologies
