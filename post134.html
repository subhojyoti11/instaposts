Caption
You and the person sitting next to you on a flight almost certainly paid different prices for what looks like an identical seat, and neither of you got a special deal or got ripped off, you just bought from two different, independently priced buckets. A single price for every seat in a cabin is the simplest possible approach, but it's a genuinely bad one for a business selling something that becomes worthless the moment a flight departs. An empty seat at takeoff isn't inventory carried over to tomorrow, it's pure lost revenue, permanently. Raising the price steadily as departure nears helps a little, but it ignores the actual signal that matters most, real-time demand, how quickly seats are actually being booked relative to how many remain. What airlines actually run instead is fare bucket pricing. Each cabin gets split into many separate inventory tiers, each holding a fixed number of seats at its own specific price. The cheapest buckets get released first, and the instant one sells out completely, that price disappears for that flight, full stop, with no more seats available at it regardless of demand. A revenue management system continuously monitors how fast each flight is actually booking compared to historical expectations, opening or closing buckets in response, sometimes multiple times a day. Every seat in that cabin remains physically identical the entire time, what changes is only which bucket a given seat happens to be assigned to at the moment you search. This is exactly why booking earlier genuinely tends to save money, it's not superstition, the cheapest buckets are the first ones to sell out, and once they're gone, later searches are simply pulling from a higher-priced tier by design. The seat was never the product with a single price. The bucket it came from always was. #aviation #revenuemanagement #pricing #businessmodels #ravenextechnologies
