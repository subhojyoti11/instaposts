Share the Air.

🎲
ALOHA
Just transmit
Goal
Send data the moment you have it, no coordination.
Focus
Any device can transmit whenever it wants.
Result
Frequent collisions, throughput collapses under load.
👂
CSMA
Listen first
Goal
Check if the channel is busy before transmitting.
Focus
A device waits for silence before it starts sending.
Result
Fewer collisions, but two devices can still start together.
🤝
CSMA/CA
Listen, then wait
Goal
Avoid collisions before they happen, not just detect them.
Focus
After sensing silence, each device waits a random backoff.
Result
Devices naturally stagger, collisions become rare.

05 / 07

The Loop, in
Pseudocode.

# One transmission attempt under CSMA/CA def send_frame(frame): while channel_busy(): wait(random_backoff()) transmit(frame) if not wait_for_ack(): # assume collision, retry with a new random backoff return send_frame(frame)

No device ever asks permission from a central authority. The network stays orderly purely because everyone follows the same random-wait rule.

07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

CSMA/CA
Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance, the protocol Wi-Fi uses to share airwaves.
Carrier sensing
Checking whether a shared communication channel is currently in use before transmitting.
Random backoff
A randomly chosen wait time used to prevent multiple devices from retrying at the exact same moment.
Collision
Two or more simultaneous transmissions overlapping and becoming unreadable at the receiver.

Caption
Every device on your Wi-Fi network is broadcasting into the exact same slice of radio spectrum, at the exact same time, and somehow they almost never talk over each other. The simplest possible approach is to just transmit whenever you have data ready, no coordination at all. That works fine with one device, and falls apart almost immediately with several, because any two devices transmitting at once produce overlapping signals that arrive unreadable at the receiver, a collision that wastes both transmissions entirely. Listening for silence before transmitting helps, but it doesn't fully solve the problem either, since two devices can both sense a silent channel at the same instant and start transmitting together anyway. What Wi-Fi actually runs is CSMA/CA, carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance. A device first listens to confirm the channel is silent, exactly like before, but then, critically, it waits a small randomly chosen amount of time before actually transmitting. Because that wait is random and different for every device, two devices that both wanted to transmit at the same instant almost never pick the identical wait time, so they naturally stagger apart instead of colliding. After a successful transmission, the receiver sends back an acknowledgment, and if the sender never hears that acknowledgment, it assumes a collision happened somewhere and retries with a fresh random wait. There is no router centrally scheduling whose turn it is to speak. Every device independently follows the same simple rule, listen, then wait a random beat, and orderly behavior emerges purely from every device following that shared protocol at once. Bluetooth solves the same shared-spectrum problem differently, hopping between frequencies instead, and older wired Ethernet used a related approach, detecting collisions after they happened rather than avoiding them beforehand. Your smart TV, laptop, and phone were never taking turns because something told them to. They were taking turns because none of them wanted to guess wrong twice. #wifi #networking #csma #wirelessprotocols #ravenextechnologies
