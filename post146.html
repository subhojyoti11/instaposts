Caption
Early consumer VR shipped with small boxes you screwed to the walls. They swept the room with infrared, sensors on the headset read that sweep, and a computer worked out where the headset was. It was extremely precise, but it meant mounting hardware, running cables, and calibrating a fixed play space. Walk out of the boxes' line of sight and tracking dropped. Then those boxes disappeared from new headsets almost all at once, and the reason is a shift in who does the locating. Instead of external hardware watching the headset, the headset watches the room. Outward-facing cameras capture the space many times a second, the system picks out distinctive visual features, corners, textures, edges, and remembers where they sit. As you move, it matches those features between frames to work out how the headset travelled. This is SLAM, simultaneous localization and mapping, the same idea a robot vacuum uses. Cameras alone stutter during fast turns or in dim light, so a small inertial sensor tracks motion in the gaps between frames, and the two streams are fused. Newer headsets add a learned model on top that predicts pose through motion blur and low texture, which is what cut the remaining dropouts. The trade was real: base stations were accurate to a fraction of a millimetre, and inside-out tracking is merely good enough. What you get back is a headset that works in any lit room with zero setup, and can be used standing in a field. The one weak spot is a blank white wall, with nothing to lock onto, the headset has to trust its motion sensors more. Nothing outside the headset was ever needed to know where you were. It just had to learn to look around and figure it out for itself. #vr #spatialcomputing #slam #tracking #ravenextechnologies
