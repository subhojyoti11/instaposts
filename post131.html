Caption
Your printer often refuses to print with real, usable ink still sitting in the cartridge, and that isn't a malfunction, it's a conservative estimate working exactly as designed. Measuring ink directly, with a sensor reading the actual fluid level inside a cartridge, would be the most accurate approach, but it adds real cost and mechanical complexity that most consumer cartridges simply skip. What ships instead is a small embedded chip that logs estimated usage from the moment the cartridge is installed, tracking pages printed and their estimated ink coverage against an expected total capacity. That's a cheap, reliable way to approximate how much ink remains, but it's an estimate, not a measurement, and estimates get built with a margin for error. That margin is deliberate. Manufacturers build in a real, unused reserve of ink before the chip ever reports the cartridge as empty, partly because print quality genuinely degrades as ink runs critically low, and partly because running a cartridge completely dry risks damaging the print head itself, a component considerably more expensive to replace than the cartridge. Stopping early, with ink still physically present, trades a small amount of waste for protecting more expensive hardware and avoiding visibly bad output. Third-party and refilled cartridges complicate this further, since the usage counter can get confused or reset incorrectly, sometimes triggering an empty warning even earlier than a genuine manufacturer cartridge would. It's also true, and widely criticized, that conservative estimates happen to encourage buying replacement cartridges sooner, a side effect that isn't purely incidental to how these systems are tuned. The cartridge was never actually empty when it said so. It was just done being trusted to keep printing. #printertech #consumertech #hardware #engineering #ravenextechnologies
