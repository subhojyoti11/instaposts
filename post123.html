Caption
Your video call almost never actually buffers on shaky wifi anymore, and it isn't because your connection got better. It's because you were never watching one fixed-quality video to begin with. Streaming a single resolution the whole way through fails in both directions, it stalls constantly on a weak connection and wastes bandwidth needlessly on a strong one, since there's no way to pick one setting that's right for a connection that keeps changing. Letting viewers manually choose a resolution themselves helps a little, but it's purely reactive, someone has to notice the problem and act on it after playback has already stuttered. What actually runs behind smooth streaming is adaptive bitrate streaming. Every video gets encoded in advance at multiple quality levels and split into short chunks, typically just a few seconds each, before anyone ever presses play. As your player downloads each chunk, it measures exactly how long that download actually took, giving it a live, constantly updating read on your real current bandwidth. Based on that measurement, plus how much video is already safely buffered ahead of your playback position, the player requests the next chunk at whichever quality level your connection can currently sustain, not the quality it started with. If your connection weakens, the next chunk quietly downgrades. If it recovers, quality climbs back up just as quietly, all without playback ever fully stopping. This is exactly why a quality drop is usually invisible, it happens cleanly at a chunk boundary rather than mid-frame, and most viewers never consciously register that anything changed at all. HLS and DASH, the two dominant streaming standards used across nearly every major platform, are both built entirely around this same chunk-and-switch architecture. You were never streaming one video.
