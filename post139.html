Three Ways to Handle
a Failing Satellite.

🚀
LAUNCH A REPLACEMENT
Build another one
Goal
Restore the service with new hardware.
Focus
Fly a fresh spacecraft, abandon the old one.
Result
Works, but slow, costly, and adds debris.
🎮
GROUND-FLOWN REPAIR
Dock it from Earth
Goal
Have operators command the docking remotely.
Focus
Every move sent over a radio link.
Result
Signal lag makes the final approach unsafe.
🛰️
AUTONOMOUS SERVICER
A robot docks itself
Goal
Send a spacecraft that docks and services on its own.
Focus
Onboard sensors handle the last-metre approach.
Result
Refuel, repair, or reboost an existing satellite.

Caption
When a satellite runs out of station-keeping fuel, or a solar array fails to deploy, or an antenna sticks, it has traditionally been finished. There was no way to reach it, so the operator built and launched a whole new spacecraft to restore the service, and the dead one stayed in orbit as a hazard to everything sharing that altitude. On-orbit servicing replaces that with a repair visit. A servicer spacecraft is launched to rendezvous with a client satellite already in orbit, dock with it, and then refuel it, replace a failed module, or take over propulsion to extend its working life or move it to a disposal orbit. The catch is that almost no satellite in orbit today was designed to be serviced, so servicers are built to grab standard features instead, most commonly the metal ring where the satellite separated from its launch vehicle. The genuinely hard part is the approach. Matching orbits from a long distance is routine, but the final few metres, closing on a target that may be slowly tumbling, cannot be flown from the ground. The round-trip radio delay between Earth and the spacecraft is long enough that a human joystick command would arrive too late to correct a drift. So the servicer carries its own cameras and laser rangefinders, continuously measures its position and orientation relative to the client, and makes the docking decisions itself. This is called rendezvous and proximity operations, and doing it autonomously is what took decades to make reliable. The payoff is twofold. A serviced satellite keeps working for years past its fuel budget, which changes the economics of building one. And every satellite that gets refuelled or towed to a graveyard orbit instead of being abandoned is one less dead object drifting through an increasingly crowded band of space. The broken satellite was never truly unreachable. There just wasn't a robot patient enough to fly the last few metres on its own. #spacetech #robotics #satellites #orbitaldebris #ravenextechnologies
