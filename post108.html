Three Ways to
Guess a Taste.

🏷️
GENRE
Category match
Goal
Recommend more of whatever category you just watched.
Focus
Matches based on fixed labels like comedy or thriller.
Result
Repetitive, misses what you actually respond to.
📄
CONTENT
Metadata match
Goal
Recommend titles with similar plot or cast metadata.
Focus
Compares descriptions, actors, and keywords directly.
Result
Better, but still blind to real audience reaction.
🧮
BEHAVIOR
Collaborative filtering
Goal
Recommend based on what similar viewers actually watched.
Focus
Finds patterns across millions of other people's history.
Result
Surfaces titles no metadata comparison would ever find.

07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

Collaborative filtering
Recommending items based on patterns across many users' behavior, not the item's own content.
Content-based filtering
Recommending items by comparing their own attributes, like genre or cast, to what you've liked.
Matrix factorization
Compressing a huge user-by-item ratings matrix into smaller hidden taste dimensions.
Cold start problem
The difficulty of recommending a new item or user with no behavioral history yet.

Caption
Netflix has never watched a single one of your shows. What it has is a giant matrix of everyone else's viewing habits, and a lot of math finding people whose taste looks statistically like yours. Sorting recommendations by genre alone is shallow, it can't distinguish a slow-burn drama you'd binge from a near-identical one you'd abandon after ten minutes, because a category label carries none of the reason you actually respond to a story. Comparing metadata like cast, plot keywords, or descriptions goes a step further, but it's still blind to the thing that actually matters, how real audiences reacted, not what a title is nominally about. What actually drives most of what you see is collaborative filtering. Every view, pause, rewatch, and abandoned episode across the entire platform becomes a data point in an enormous shared matrix of viewers and titles. Instead of analyzing what a show is about, the system finds other viewers whose viewing pattern closely resembles yours, and surfaces titles those similar viewers loved that you haven't seen yet. Behind the scenes, that entire matrix gets compressed through matrix factorization into a small set of hidden taste dimensions, discovered statistically, never labeled or named by a human at any point. The system genuinely doesn't know what a movie is about. It only knows a pattern, people who watched what you watched also tended to love this, and that pattern turns out to predict your taste better than reading a plot summary ever could. Around eighty percent of what people watch on Netflix reportedly comes from these recommendations rather than active search. Brand new titles face a real cold start problem, with no viewing history yet to draw on, which is why they initially lean on content-based signals until real behavioral data accumulates. Spotify's Discover Weekly and Amazon's "customers also bought" run on the exact same underlying principle. Your taste was never described to the system.
