07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

RLHF
Training a policy using a reward model learned from human preferences, instead of a hand-coded formula.
Reward model
A model trained to predict how a human would rate a behavior, based on prior pairwise comparisons.
Pairwise preference
Choosing which of two behaviors is better, a judgment humans make reliably even without an exact score.
Reward hacking
When a policy exploits a narrow reward function to score well while behaving unsafely.

Caption
Nobody can write down a formula for "polite." You cannot hand a robot an equation that fully defines a comfortable approach distance, an acceptable reaching speed near someone's face, or what counts as startling versus gentle. Yet robots are increasingly expected to get exactly that right. RLHF is how they learn it anyway. The old approach was to hand-code the rules directly, engineers enumerating acceptable distances, speeds, and forbidden motions one by one. It breaks constantly, because the real world always produces a scenario nobody explicitly anticipated. Training in simulation against a hand-coded reward function doesn't fully fix this either. A simulator can score whether a task was completed, or how much energy or time it took, but it has no way to judge whether a motion actually felt safe or comfortable to a human. Left to optimize a narrow formula like that, robots reliably find reward hacking shortcuts, technically maximizing the score while behaving in ways nobody intended. Reinforcement learning from human feedback sidesteps the entire problem by never asking anyone to write the formula. Instead, the robot generates two different motions for the same task, and a human evaluator simply watches both and picks whichever one felt more appropriate, no numeric scoring required, just a preference. Thousands of these pairwise comparisons train a separate reward model that learns to predict how a human would rate any new motion, capturing nuance no engineer could have written down directly. The robot's actual policy is then trained to maximize that learned reward model, effectively optimizing directly against human judgment rather than a proxy for it. The results show up exactly where hand-coded rules failed. Robots trained this way learn comfortable approach distances and appropriate motion speed around people purely from evaluator preferences, and because the reward itself reflects genuine human judgment rather than a narrow formula, they are far less prone to the strange, technically-correct shortcuts reward hacking produces. We could never fully specify what "safe" physical behavior means. It turns out we didn't need to, we only needed to reliably recognize it when we saw it. #reinforcementlearning #rlhf #robotics #humanrobotinteraction #ravenextechnologies
