Caption
Delivering a single package by drone has been possible for a long time. Doing it thousands of times a day across suburban neighbourhoods is a different problem, and the drone itself was never the hard part. Two things had to be solved around it. The first is regulation. Early rules for commercial drones require a human operator to keep the aircraft in direct sight at all times, and often a second person as a dedicated observer. That is safe, but it means one or two people per drone, which cannot scale to a real delivery network. The unlock is permission to fly beyond visual line of sight, so one operator can oversee many flights from a control room instead of a field. Alongside that sits the need for traffic management in low airspace, the band below where crewed aircraft fly, where until recently nothing coordinated who flies where. The second is onboard autonomy, and this is where computer vision matters. A preplanned GPS route is fine over open ground but blind to anything new, a van parked where the map showed clear road, a child in the yard, a thin power line strung between two poles that is nearly invisible against a bright sky. Modern delivery drones carry cameras and run detection models on board that spot these obstacles in real time, hold or reroute around them, and scan the drop zone to choose a clear patch before lowering the package on a tether. Put together, a licensed corridor through managed airspace, permission to fly unwatched, and a drone that sees and reacts for itself, one operator can supervise a fleet rather than babysit an aircraft. The multirotor that can lift a parcel was solved years ago. Suburban drone delivery was waiting on the rules that let it fly unwatched and the vision that lets it land safely. #drones #logistics #computervision #aviation #ravenextechnologies
