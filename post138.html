Caption
A tiny lost tracker with no GPS chip, no cellular antenna, and no internet connection of its own can still be found anywhere, because it was never actually trying to find itself. A tracker small and cheap enough to clip onto a keychain has no room for the battery or antenna a real GPS and cellular connection would require, that combination is what makes phones expensive and bulky in the first place. Relying purely on direct Bluetooth to your own phone works fine at home, but Bluetooth's range is only a few dozen meters, useless the moment the tracker is genuinely lost somewhere far from you. What actually finds a lost tracker is a crowdsourced mesh network built from millions of other people's phones already out in the world. The tag constantly emits a low-power, encrypted Bluetooth signal. Any participating phone that happens to pass within range detects that signal automatically, with its owner never knowing or doing anything at all, and anonymously relays the tag's approximate location upstream. Critically, that relaying phone has no way to identify whose tag it just detected, the location report is encrypted specifically so that only the tag's actual owner, holding the correct private key, can ever decrypt and see where it was picked up. Everyone else in the chain is just an unwitting, anonymous courier for a signal they can't read. Coverage under this system depends entirely on how many participating devices happen to pass near the lost item, not on the tracker's own power at all, which is why a tag lost somewhere busy gets found quickly while one lost somewhere truly remote might not report for a while. Preventing this same mesh from being repurposed to covertly track a person, rather than an object, was one of the hardest parts of designing it responsibly. Your lost keys were never broadcasting to you directly. Strangers' phones were quietly doing that for you, without ever knowing whose keys they were. #privacy #bluetooth #iot #distributedsystems #ravenextechnologies
