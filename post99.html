Randomizing
Every Episode.

# Domain randomization, one training episode def new_episode(sim): sim.friction = random_range(0.4, 1.2) sim.mass = random_range(0.8, 1.3) sim.light = random_choice(LIGHT_PRESETS) sim.texture = random_choice(TEXTURE_SET) # the policy never sees the same world twice

If a policy survives thousands of randomized physics variants, the real world just becomes one more variant it already handles.

07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

Sim2real gap
The performance drop when a policy trained in simulation meets the real world's imperfect, unpredictable physics.
Domain randomization
Varying simulated physics and visuals every episode so the policy generalizes instead of overfitting to one exact simulator.
System identification
Measuring a real robot's actual physical parameters and feeding them back into the simulator to make it more accurate.
Zero-shot transfer
Deploying a sim-trained policy directly to real hardware with no additional training on the physical robot.

Caption
A robot can learn to walk perfectly in a physics simulator and fall over the instant it touches real ground. That gap between simulated and physical performance has a name, the sim2real gap, and closing it is one of the most practically important problems in modern robotics. The root cause is that no physics engine is a perfect model of reality. Friction, contact dynamics, and motor response are all approximations, and simulated sensors are far cleaner than the noisy, imperfect ones on real hardware. A policy trained against one exact simulated world can end up quietly exploiting quirks of that simulation that simply do not exist in the physical one, producing behavior that looks flawless in training and fails immediately on a real robot. Three techniques work together to close that gap. Domain randomization trains the policy against thousands of varied physics conditions instead of one fixed simulation, randomizing friction, mass, lighting, and textures every single episode, so the policy learns to be robust across a wide range rather than perfectly tuned to a single, unrealistic setup. System identification works the other direction: measuring a real robot's actual motor delays and friction coefficients, then feeding those precise numbers back into the simulator to make the simulation itself more accurate from the start. Fine-tuning closes whatever gap remains after both of those, adapting the sim-trained policy using a comparatively small amount of real-world data, far cheaper than training from scratch on physical hardware. What makes this practical at all is simulation speed. Platforms like NVIDIA's Isaac Gym run thousands of copies of the same robot in parallel directly on GPU hardware, compressing years of equivalent physical practice into a few hours of compute time. Most modern legged locomotion policies, walking quadrupeds and humanoids alike, are trained almost entirely this way, then deployed to physical hardware with little or no additional tuning. None of the three techniques alone fully closes the reality gap. Combined, they are how a policy that has never touched a real motor ends up controlling one successfully on the first try. #sim2real #robotics #reinforcementlearning #nvidiaisaac #embodiedai #ravenextechnologies
