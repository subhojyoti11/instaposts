Quick
Reference.

Inductive loop
A wire coil embedded in a road surface that detects vehicles by sensing changes in its magnetic field.
Actuated signal
A traffic light that changes based on real-time detected demand, not a fixed timer alone.
Inductance
A measure of how a coil's magnetic field responds to nearby conductive material.
Loop detector controller
The roadside electronics that continuously monitor loop inductance and relay vehicle presence.

Caption
That empty intersection at 2am doesn't turn green because of luck or a hidden camera watching you. There's a ring of wire buried in the road, and your car's metal body just tripped it. Cycling every direction on a fixed schedule is the simplest way to run a traffic light, and it's also the most wasteful, handing out green lights to empty roads and red lights to cars that are actually waiting, entirely regardless of who's there. Overhead cameras improve on that by visually detecting waiting vehicles in each lane, but cameras need decent lighting and a clear line of sight to work reliably, which makes fog, glare, heavy rain, and plain darkness genuine failure conditions. What sits under most actuated intersections instead is an inductive loop, a coil of wire cut into a rectangular slot in the road surface and sealed just beneath the asphalt. Roadside electronics drive a small current through that loop, creating a steady, constant magnetic field. When your car's metal body passes over it and sits there waiting, that mass of metal measurably changes the loop's inductance, shifting the magnetic field just enough for the controller to detect. That shift alone is the trigger, no image, no camera, no visual identification of what kind of vehicle is even present, just a measurable disruption in a field that would otherwise stay constant. The controller reads that shift and factors it directly into the signal's next change, which is exactly why an empty-looking intersection can sit red for cross traffic and then suddenly go green the moment your car rolls to a stop over the buried loop. Some cities cut smaller, more sensitive loops specifically tuned to pick up bicycles, which carry far less metal than a car, and newer intersections increasingly pair these decades-old loops with radar units for redundancy. Nothing was ever watching the road. The system was only ever waiting to feel a change in a magnetic field it had been measuring the whole time. #trafficengineering #sensors #smartcities #infrastructure #ravenextechnologies
