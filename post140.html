Caption
Paying off a credit card balance in full doesn't move your credit score the same day, and it's not because the system is slow or broken, it's because credit scoring was never designed to update in real time to begin with. Your bank isn't the one calculating that number at all, it lives with separate credit bureaus that receive data from issuers rather than tracking your account directly. Reporting every single transaction to those bureaus the instant it happens would technically be possible, but no card issuer's infrastructure is actually built that way, because scoring itself was never designed to consume a live, continuously updating feed. What issuers actually do is compile one snapshot of your account, balance, payment status, utilization, as of a specific date at the end of each billing cycle, typically around every thirty days, and submit that single batch to the major bureaus once per cycle. Only once that batch arrives does a bureau's scoring model recalculate anything at all, which means the number you see reflects whatever your account looked like on that one reporting date, not whatever it looks like the moment you check it. Pay off a large balance the day after your statement closes, and that payment simply won't show up in a score calculation until the next cycle's batch goes out weeks later. There's a real practical upside buried in this though. Because utilization gets reported as of the statement date specifically, paying down a balance before that date, rather than after, lets a lower balance get captured in that snapshot sooner. And because each of the three major bureaus receives its own separate batch on its own timing, the identical account can genuinely show slightly different numbers across all three at once. Your score was never lagging behind reality by accident. It was only ever built to check in once a month, not to watch continuously. #creditscore #personalfinance #fintech #howthingswork #ravenextechnologies
