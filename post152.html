Caption
A new engineer joining most teams doesn't actually learn the system from documentation, they learn it from asking a senior engineer the same questions previous new hires already asked, because the real architecture only ever lived in someone's head. That pattern is common, and it's expensive in ways that don't always show up on a spreadsheet. Onboarding takes longer than it should, because piecing together an undocumented system from scattered conversations is slower than reading a clear explanation once. Critical knowledge concentrates in one or two people, which becomes a real risk the moment they're on vacation, in back-to-back meetings, or leave the team entirely. And without written architecture to point to, every question becomes a synchronous interruption instead of something someone could have looked up. A wiki page written during a rare quiet week helps briefly, but it drifts out of date within months and gets trusted less every time it turns out to be wrong. What actually solves this is documentation written with the same standard this library is built on, mapping the system as it genuinely works today rather than as it was originally designed, interviewing the engineers who hold the tribal knowledge that never made it into any document, and writing the result clearly enough that people actually read it instead of skimming past it. The goal isn't a document nobody opens again. It's architecture documentation your team can actually maintain after the engagement ends, structured well enough that keeping it current doesn't feel like a chore, and clear enough that a new engineer can genuinely learn the system from it instead of interrupting someone else to explain it one more time. The problem was never that nobody understood the system. It's that the understanding lived in exactly one place, somebody's memory, instead of somewhere the whole team could actually reach it. #techdocumentation #softwareengineering #engineeringconsulting #systemdesign #ravenextechnologies
