Three Ways to Solve
a Diff. Equation.

🔢
DIGITAL STEPPING
Step by step
Goal
Approximate the curve one time-step at a time.
Focus
Millions of rounded arithmetic operations.
Result
Exact to many digits, slow and power-hungry.
⚙️
SOLVER CHIP
Accelerate the stepping
Goal
Run the same method faster in hardware.
Focus
Parallel digital arithmetic units.
Result
Faster, still discretized and rounding.
〰️
ANALOG CIRCUIT
Be the equation
Goal
Let the circuit obey the equation directly.
Focus
Voltages, integrators, and summers wired to match.
Result
The answer evolves continuously, in real time.

Set It Up,
Let It Run.

Each variable is assigned a voltage on a wire
Op-amps are wired as integrators and summers to match the equation
Initial conditions are set and the circuit is released
The output voltage traces the solution in real time, no stepping

The circuit isn't calculating the answer. Its own electrical behaviour is governed by the same differential equation, so the solution just appears as a voltage over time.

Analog Computing
A circuit whose physics obeys the equation, instead of a program approximating it.
Continuous Variable
A voltage standing in for a number, not a string of discrete rounded bits.
Op-Amp Integrator
The wired component that performs calculus, continuously, not step by step.
Time-Stepping
Slicing time into tiny digital steps, the slow, rounding-prone method analog skips.

Caption
A digital computer solves a differential equation by pretending time moves in discrete jumps. It slices the timeline into tiny steps and, at each step, performs a batch of arithmetic to estimate how every variable changes. For a large system, a fluid simulation, a power-grid model, a network of coupled equations, that is millions or billions of operations, each carrying a small rounding error that accumulates, and numerical stability often forces the steps to be even smaller, which makes it slower still. An analog computer does not step through time at all. Each variable in the problem is represented by a continuous physical quantity, usually a voltage on a wire. Operational amplifiers are then wired into configurations that perform integration and summation, and the wiring is arranged so that the circuit's own electrical behaviour is described by the same differential equation you are trying to solve. You set the initial conditions as starting voltages, release the circuit, and the output voltage simply traces out the solution as it evolves, in real time, at the speed of the electronics, with no discretization and no per-step rounding. The revival happening now is not about replacing digital computers. It is about analog blocks, often reconfigurable, sitting on a chip next to a digital host and acting as an accelerator for the kinds of math they are good at: systems of differential equations, real-time control, and increasingly the multiply-accumulate operations at the heart of neural networks, done in a resistor crossbar using basic circuit laws. The limitation is precision. An analog result is typically good to a few digits, set by electrical noise and component tolerances, not the fifteen-plus digits a digital float gives you. For problems where that is enough, the speed and energy advantage can be large. The circuit was never computing the answer. It was built so that its own physics had no choice but to obey the same equation. #analogcomputing #hardware #computing #differentialequations #ravenextechnologies
