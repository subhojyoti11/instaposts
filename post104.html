It's Not Checking
Your Spelling.

Binary dictionary lookup
Early spellcheckers only flagged words missing from a fixed dictionary, offering no ranked guess at all.
No context awareness
A word-list checker treats "there" and "their" identically, it has no idea which one your sentence needs.
One typo, wrong fix

03 / 07

Three Ways to
Guess a Word.

📖
DICTIONARY
Word list
Goal
Flag any word missing from a fixed dictionary.
Focus
Exact match against a static list of known words.
Result
Catches typos, blind to meaning or context.
🔤
EDIT DISTANCE
Nearest spelling
Goal
Find the closest correctly spelled word to what you typed.
Focus
Counts letter insertions, deletions, swaps to the nearest match.
Result
Fixes typos, still guesses without context.
✍️
PROBABILITY
Language model
Goal
Predict the most likely next word given everything before it.
Focus
Ranks candidates by probability learned from huge text corpora.
Result
Suggests, corrects, and predicts, using context.

The Loop, in
Pseudocode.

# One keystroke tick in a predictive keyboard def suggest_word(typed, context): candidates = nearby_spellings(typed) ranked = rank_by_probability(candidates, context) if ranked[0].prob > THRESHOLD: return ranked[0] # silently replaces what you typed return typed

The "wrong" autocorrect isn't a spelling failure. It's a probability model confidently betting on the wrong word for your context.

07 / 07

Quick
Reference.

N-gram model
Predicting a word's probability from the two or three words that came immediately before it.
Edit distance
Counting the minimum letter changes needed to turn one word into another.
Language model
A system trained to estimate how likely a sequence of words is to appear together.
Autocorrect threshold
The probability score a suggested word must clear before it silently replaces what you typed.

Caption
Every time your keyboard finishes a word before you do, it isn't checking spelling. It's running a bet on which word is statistically most likely to come next, and it happens to also fix your typos along the way. The earliest spellcheckers worked off a plain dictionary lookup, a word either existed in a fixed list or it didn't. That catches obvious nonsense but offers zero help ranking which correction you actually meant, and it has no concept of context at all, "there" and "their" look identical to a system that only checks whether a word exists. Edit distance improved that by measuring how many letters would need to change, add, or delete to turn your typed word into a real one, then picking whichever known word is closest. That fixes far more typos, but it still guesses blind. It has no idea whether you're writing a normal word in a sentence or something your keyboard has learned to override for you. What actually runs today is a language model trained on billions of real sentences, estimating the probability of every possible next word given the two or three words you just typed. Every keystroke reranks that whole list of candidates in real time, combining spelling proximity with contextual probability, and the top-ranked guess gets silently substituted the instant its score clears a threshold, whether you asked for it or not. That's also exactly why autocorrect fails in such a specific, memeable way. It isn't confused about spelling, it's confidently betting on the statistically likely word for people in general, not the specific word you actually meant. The same probability engine powers swipe typing too, reconstructing an entire word from an imprecise dragged finger path by comparing it against the most probable candidates, not a perfect key by key trace. Correcting a typo was never the hard part. Guessing your next word before you've finished typing it, that's the trick. #autocorrect #nlp #languagemodels #machinelearning #ravenextechnologies
