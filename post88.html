Quick
Reference.

Leader election
Followers promote themselves to candidate after a timeout and request votes to become the new leader.
Log replication
The leader appends entries and replicates them to followers before considering a write committed.
Quorum
Any decision needs agreement from a majority of nodes, which is why the cluster tolerates losing up to half.
Term
A logical clock that increases with every election, used to detect and reject messages from a stale former leader.

Caption
A cluster of five servers can lose two of them at the exact same moment and still agree, unanimously and correctly, on what happened next. That guarantee comes from an algorithm designed specifically so a normal engineering team could actually build it correctly. Paxos proved distributed consensus was mathematically possible back in 1989. It was also so difficult to explain that its own author eventually wrote a second paper just to clarify the first one. Teams building real systems on top of Paxos routinely got subtle edge cases wrong, not because the algorithm was flawed but because it was nearly impossible to reason about correctly under deadline pressure. Raft was built backward from that failure. Same formal guarantees as Paxos, but designed from day one around a single explicit goal: understandability. The core idea is a strict leader. At any moment, exactly one server in the cluster is the leader, and every write request goes through it first before being replicated out to the followers. Followers expect a heartbeat from the leader every few milliseconds. If that heartbeat stops arriving, meaning the leader crashed or got network-partitioned away, a follower waits out a randomized timeout and promotes itself to candidate, requesting votes from the rest of the cluster. Win votes from a majority, not everyone, just a majority, and you become the new leader for a new term. That single word, majority, is why the cluster survives losing up to half its nodes. A five-node cluster only needs three nodes to agree on anything, which means it keeps running correctly even after two full node failures. Every decision, from electing a leader to committing a write to the replicated log, follows the same rule: get a majority, and you are safe. This is not an academic algorithm. Kubernetes stores its entire cluster state in etcd, which is Raft underneath, and that is exactly why the control plane keeps functioning through individual node failures. Consul and CockroachDB use the same mechanism to replicate configuration and transactional data with strong consistency guarantees.
