Caption
Controlling weeds on a farm has traditionally meant spraying herbicide across the entire field, even though most of that chemical lands on bare soil or on the crop itself rather than on a weed. Decades of using the same few herbicides this way has bred weed populations that simply survive them, and a heavier dose is both expensive and environmentally worse. The obvious alternative, a crew walking the rows with hoes, does not scale to a large modern farm. Autonomous weeding moves the unit of decision from the field to the individual plant. A rover drives slowly down each crop row, and a downward-facing camera photographs every plant that passes underneath. An onboard vision model, trained on huge numbers of labeled images, classifies each one as crop or weed in a few milliseconds, using leaf shape, size, color, and position relative to the seed line. When it identifies a weed, the machine acts on that exact spot, either firing a short laser pulse that boils the weed's growing point, or dropping a small mechanical blade, while the crop plant a few centimeters away is left completely untouched. Drones do the reconnaissance. Flown over the field ahead of the rover, they carry multispectral cameras that reveal weed patches and areas of crop stress well before those are visible from ground level, so the ground machine knows where the pressure is worst. The result on some trials is a reduction in herbicide use of around ninety percent or more, with the remaining weeds removed mechanically rather than chemically. The cost moves from ongoing chemical purchases to a capital machine, and the soil, the crop, and nearby water get far less exposure. The robot was never really weeding the field. It was making one small yes or no decision, crop or weed, a few hundred times a minute, and acting only on the answers that said weed. #agtech #robotics #precisionagriculture #ai #ravenextechnologies
